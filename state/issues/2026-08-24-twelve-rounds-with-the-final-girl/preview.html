Twelve Rounds With The Final Girl

the week I got destroyed on the mitts, watched The Odyssey three times, and found a slasher that understands comebacks nobody asked for

2026-08-28 · Joshua Laurie
horrorboxingrunning

Three months into boxing now, and it has turned into a genuine revelation, the kind of thing I didn't expect to still be doing on a random Tuesday morning let alone still thinking about on the walk home. Most of the people reading this off the Facebook link have no idea I even do this, so consider it out now.

Yesterday's mitts session might be the funniest thing that's happened to me in the gym so far. I was doing slow, intentional bag work between rounds, working on form rather than power, when another boxer's coach clocked me from across the room and decided, loudly, in full earshot of everyone else training that day, that I looked sad staring at the bag. He pulled me straight into a mitts session to fix that. Three rounds of three minutes each, which felt manageable, genuinely fine, I thought we were done. Then I looked up for the fourth and final round and the clock was already ticking down from just under four minutes, not the three I'd braced for. Turned out he'd quietly set the whole thing for five minutes and just let me find that out in real time, reminding me the entire way through that every hand I dropped would be punished. I spent the last two minutes just trying to survive, hands somewhere near my chin through pure fear rather than technique.

That's roughly the state I've been watching films in this week too, tired in a way that isn't really about the body. It's easy to romanticise the grind when you're not currently in it, and this week's watching kept circling back to the gap between the version of yourself who thinks they've got it all figured out and the one who actually has to show up. I spent a good chunk of my twenties convinced I had it sorted, mostly by ignoring whatever I was actually responsible for and letting the next man in line deal with it. Three months of daily bag work has been the opposite lesson. Showing up is the whole job. Nobody else does your rounds for you.

Spider-Man: Brand New Day (2026)

Tom Holland remains my favourite Spider-Man, full stop, and watching him graduate from high-school Spidey to college Spidey across these films has been one of the better character arcs this character's ever gotten on screen. Brand New Day leans hard into comeback, rebirth, finding yourself again, and that line, 'I'm both', is doing a lot of work in a film that otherwise moves fast and jokes constantly.

There's something in that 'I'm both' that landed the same way pulling on a mouthguard does after a day of pretending to be a functioning adult at a desk. You're not becoming someone else in the gym, you're just letting the other half breathe for a while. This film understands that duality better than most in the genre manage.

Quick aside because I can't not mention it: Jean Grey is genuinely bad here, and I mean that about her, not the writing. What she actually does in this film is horrible, properly unsettling in a franchise that mostly deals in city-block property damage. The script gives her the space to be that, resolved fast, sure, but landed with real weight while it's happening. Holland's performance and the relatable charm running through the whole thing are exactly why this is tracking to be one of the highest-grossing films of all time, and deservedly so.

The Odyssey (2026)

This is Nolan's crack at Homer, and whatever else gets said about it, the film understands that the point of the Odyssey was never really the monsters. It's the distance. I saw it three times, twice in a regular cinema and once on IMAX, and that third viewing might have been the real one. The sheer vastness of it on 70mm was dazzling in a way the first two only hinted at. Nolan clearly built this for the format, using every inch of the image to sell what a ten-year journey home actually costs a person. The man is basically a franchise unto himself at this point, and for my money this might be his best film yet.

The thing that's had me thinking isn't the sea voyage, though, it's a different kind of loop I've fallen into lately. I've been messing around with Claude Code, sitting down for what's supposed to be a quick fix and looking up five hours later having built something else entirely, having somehow done a full day's work without touching the actual task in front of me. That's the Odyssey's real trick too. Everyone remembers the monsters. The story is actually the twenty-year distraction from getting home, dressed up as heroism the whole way there.

Obsession (2025)

Obsession is a properly great thriller. The sound design and pacing are doing so much heavy lifting that by the time anything violent or supernatural actually happens, you're already strung out from everything that came before it. Every section earns its own small payoff without straining for one, which for an independent horror film feels almost like cheating by refusing to follow the usual playbook. My read is it succeeds precisely because it never once worries about hitting blockbuster beats. It's interested in telling its story properly and respecting whoever's watching it, and that respect is rare enough to stand out immediately.

It sits in my head the same way a coach's threat to punish every dropped hand does. Obsession runs on that exact logic. Once you understand there's a cost attached to every small lapse, you can't stop bracing for it, and the film knows exactly how long to make you wait before it finally lands the shot.

Tony (2026)

A Bourdain biopic that leans hard into kitchen chaos as its own kind of combat sport, and honestly it works, because a service rush and the final thirty seconds of a close round have the same texture. Heat, noise, somebody yelling numbers at you, no time to think, only react.

Small, embarrassing confession, there is a line cook in a bandana who appears for maybe six minutes of screen time and I would have watched an entire spinoff about him alone. That's not a boxing observation, I just needed to say it somewhere. What the film gets right is the grind underneath the glamour, the burns and the double shifts that never make the highlight reel, which is a very gym-floor slash kitchen-floor way of telling a story.

The Final Girl Comes Back for One More Round

The setup for Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma is almost administrative in how bluntly it lays out the problem. After years of slapdash sequels and waning fandom, the Camp Miasma slasher franchise gets handed to an enthusiastic young director for resurrection, and that director becomes fixated on one thing above all else: getting the original film's reclusive final girl to come back and survive the sequel alongside her. It's described as a riveting two-hander centered on a passionate writer hired to reinvigorate their favourite horror franchise with the help of that now-reclusive survivor, and the film knows exactly what it's doing by making the survivor herself the subject rather than the punchline at the end of act three.

That's the part that stuck with me on the bag this week, because the final girl has always been horror's version of the fighter who's still standing in round twelve, not because she hit hardest but because she absorbed everything thrown at her and simply refused to go down. Nobody remembers the exact combination that ended it. They remember that she was still upright when the credits rolled. Camp Miasma takes that trope and does something almost cruel with it, which is drag the woman who already survived the worst night of her life back into the exact same arena for a rematch she never asked for and clearly doesn't want.

That is, unglamorously, the maths of a comeback fight. Nobody schedules a comeback because the body's fully healed and raring to go. You get the call because somebody needs a name on the poster, and because you're still the one who can go the distance even when everything in you would rather not. The corner doesn't care that you already did this once. The gym doesn't care that you told everyone you were done. If you can still take a shot and get back up, eventually somebody's going to ask you to prove it again, and there's a particular exhaustion in agreeing to it that has nothing to do with fitness and everything to do with the fact that your body remembers exactly what this costs.

What makes the film sharper than a straightforward slasher cash-in is that it's framed as a queer director becoming obsessed with casting the original film's final girl, which tips both women into genuine psychological and, per some descriptions, sexual chaos, meaning the pursuit of the survivor becomes as unsettling as the killer she originally escaped. That's a smart bit of structure. The monster in the woods was never really the point of the first film, and the monster of expectation, the fandom, the sequel machine, the need to relive the trauma for an audience, isn't really the point of this one either. It's the same trick a comeback fight pulls on a boxer. The other guy in the ring is almost incidental. The real opponent is the version of yourself that already quietly decided you were finished, and now has to be talked out of that decision one more time.

I think about journeyman fighters more than I probably should, the ones who never got the title shot but kept taking fights because that's the job, because somebody's got to be the twelfth round for somebody else's undefeated record, and there's a dignity in that which the film understands about its final girl too. She's not coming back because she's triumphant. She's coming back because she's capable, and being capable when you'd rather not be is its own quiet horror, one that has nothing to do with a killer in a mask and everything to do with what it costs to be the person who can still take it.

That's the week. Hands wrapped, legs tired, a coach out there somewhere still convinced I look sad on the bags. Back at it before the sun's properly up. See you in the next round.
