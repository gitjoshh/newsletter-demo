Twelve Rounds With The Final Girl

the week in gloves, roadwork, and horror films that refuse to let anyone stay retired

Six rounds on the bag this morning, the last two with my hands too tired to keep them up where they belonged, which is its own kind of horror film if you think about it. Cold outside, that grey almost-light where the road is just you and whichever dog decided today was the day to test you. That's where most of my thinking happens lately, on the roadwork, on the walk to the gym, in the gap between rounds when the corner is yelling something you only half hear.

This week's watching turned out to be a strange double bill of exhaustion. A superhero who can't stop looking wounded even when he's winning. An odyssey that's basically one long run home. A film about a man so consumed by guilt he can't tap out of his own head. A chef biopic that made we want to eat an entire kitchen. And then the one that actually earns the horror tag and gave me the deep-dive, a slasher sequel that drags its original survivor back into the ring for a fight she thought she'd already finished. That's the thread this week. Nobody in any of these films gets to just walk away clean. Neither do I, most days, but that's the job.

Spider-Man: Brand New Day (2026)

There's a version of this character who's fun because the stakes are low enough to let him crack jokes mid-swing. This one isn't that. He's still cracking jokes, technically, but there's a heaviness sitting behind his eyes the whole runtime, like a fighter who's won the last six on points and still can't sleep right.

I kept thinking about the kind of exhaustion that has nothing to do with the actual damage. You can walk out of sparring with barely a mark on you and still feel like you got hit by a bus, because the fight that hurt wasn't the one in the ring. Peter's got that same tell. Big wins, small joy. I don't know if the film means to be about that specifically but it's the thing I carried out of the cinema and onto my next roadwork loop, turning it over instead of counting my splits.

The Odyssey (2026)

This is Christopher Nolan's crack at Homer, apparently shot with a scale and cast list that suggests nobody involved was thinking small, and whatever else you say about it, the film understands that the point of the Odyssey was never the monsters. It's the distance. Ten years to get home is basically the longest, cruellest fun run in literature.

I've never rowed a boat away from a war but I have gone out for what was supposed to be a six mile loop and come back two hours later because my legs decided the route wasn't done with me yet. That specific brand of stubbornness, the kind where turning back feels worse than finishing wrong, is the whole engine of this story. Odysseus isn't fast. He's just the guy who doesn't stop.

Obsession (2025)

This one's a proper piece of guilt-horror, the kind that gets under your skin because it's not built on a monster, it's built on a decision somebody made and can't undo. I watched it the same night I'd spent forty minutes replaying a single exchange from sparring in my head, the one where I dropped my hand for half a second and paid for it, and the parallel wasn't subtle to me even if nobody else would clock it.

There's a kind of obsession that's productive, the tape study, the drilling the same combination until it's boring, and then there's the kind that just eats you from the inside because you can't let a mistake be a mistake. This film lives entirely in that second category and it's genuinely unsettling for it. Good work, uncomfortable to sit with, exactly what it should be.

Tony (2026)

A Bourdain biopic that leans hard into kitchen chaos as its own kind of combat sport, and honestly it works, because a service rush and the final thirty seconds of a close round have the same texture. Heat, noise, somebody yelling numbers at you, no time to think, only react.

Small, embarrassing confession, there is a line cook in a bandana who appears for maybe six minutes of screen time and I would have watched an entire spinoff about him alone. That's not a boxing observation, I just needed to say it somewhere. What the film gets right is the grind underneath the glamour, the burns and the double shifts that never make the highlight reel, which is a very gym-floor way of telling a story.

The Final Girl Comes Back for One More Round

The setup for Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma is almost administrative in how bluntly it lays out the problem. After years of slapdash sequels and waning fandom, the Camp Miasma slasher franchise gets handed to an enthusiastic young director for resurrection, and that director becomes fixated on one thing above all else: getting the original film's reclusive final girl to come back and survive the sequel alongside her. It's described as a riveting two-hander centered on a passionate writer hired to reinvigorate their favourite horror franchise with the help of that now-reclusive survivor, and the film knows exactly what it's doing by making the survivor herself the subject rather than the punchline at the end of act three.

That's the part that stuck with me on the bag this week, because the final girl has always been horror's version of the fighter who's still standing in round twelve, not because she hit hardest but because she absorbed everything thrown at her and simply refused to go down. Nobody remembers the exact combination that ended it. They remember that she was still upright when the credits rolled. Camp Miasma takes that trope and does something almost cruel with it, which is drag the woman who already survived the worst night of her life back into the exact same arena for a rematch she never asked for and clearly doesn't want.

That is, unglamorously, the maths of a comeback fight. Nobody schedules a comeback because the body's fully healed and raring to go. You get the call because somebody needs a name on the poster, and because you're still the one who can go the distance even when everything in you would rather not. The corner doesn't care that you already did this once. The gym doesn't care that you told everyone you were done. If you can still take a shot and get back up, eventually somebody's going to ask you to prove it again, and there's a particular exhaustion in agreeing to it that has nothing to do with fitness and everything to do with the fact that your body remembers exactly what this costs.

What makes the film sharper than a straightforward slasher cash-in is that it's framed as a queer director becoming obsessed with casting the original film's final girl, which tips both women into genuine psychological and, per some descriptions, sexual chaos, meaning the pursuit of the survivor becomes as unsettling as the killer she originally escaped. That's a smart bit of structure. The monster in the woods was never really the point of the first film, and the monster of expectation, the fandom, the sequel machine, the need to relive the trauma for an audience, isn't really the point of this one either. It's the same trick a comeback fight pulls on a boxer. The other guy in the ring is almost incidental. The real opponent is the version of yourself that already quietly decided you were finished, and now has to be talked out of that decision one more time.

I think about journeyman fighters more than I probably should, the ones who never got the title shot but kept taking fights because that's the job, because somebody's got to be the twelfth round for somebody else's undefeated record, and there's a dignity in that which the film understands about its final girl too. She's not coming back because she's triumphant. She's coming back because she's capable, and being capable when you'd rather not be is its own quiet horror, one that has nothing to do with a killer in a mask and everything to do with what it costs to be the person who can still take it.

That's the week. Hands wrapped, legs tired, six films deep and only one of them actually scared me, though not in the way it meant to. Back on the road tomorrow before the sun's properly up. See you in the next round.
