Run For Your Life

The Jab Nobody Applauds

This week's watching, from an anvil lawsuit to a killer who never raises his voice

2026-09-09 · Joshua Laurie
horrorboxingrunning

Six rounds on the bag this morning, the last two on legs that were already gone from a run I probably didn't need to do at that pace. There's a specific kind of stupid where you decide the 5k warm-up should also be the workout, and by round four your hands are heavy for reasons that have nothing to do with your hands. I mention it because this week's watching kept circling the same idea from different angles: the difference between force and patience, between the punch that lands clean and the one that just keeps arriving until something gives. Some of that came from a coyote suing a hardware conglomerate. Most of it came from a killer who never has to raise his voice.

Coyote vs. Acme (2026)

I did not expect a legal drama about a cartoon coyote's personal injury case against the Acme Corporation to be the funniest thing I watched all week, and yet here we are. There's a real deposition-room rhythm to it, lawyers grinding through decades of anvils and rocket skates like case law, and something about that plodding procedural pace landed for me on a day when my own session was mostly just repetition. Boxing rewards the unglamorous rep long before it rewards the highlight. This film gets that a slapstick body count is basically an occupational injury log if you sit with it long enough.

I don't know what genre box this belongs in and I don't think the film does either, which is part of the charm. It's Albuquerque as a courtroom, not a desert. Wile E. Coyote as a plaintiff, not a punchline. I laughed more than I expected and thought about workplace liability more than I wanted to.

Buddy (2026)

Great, another fat orange monster that touches kids and creeps on grown women, and I'm supposed to find this frightening instead of just deeply familiar from certain gyms I've trained at. Casper Kelly's practical-effects throwback takes the beloved children's-show mascot and lets the rot show through the felt, and it works because it never winks at you about it. The thing is genuinely gross in a way that reminded me of sparring someone much bigger than you for the first time. You know exactly what's coming and it still gets you.

There's a puppet-horror lineage here and this one earns its spot in it. I'd put it up against most of what's come through that particular subgenre in the last decade. The comedy and the dread are doing the same rep, over and over, until you stop laughing.

Obsession (2025)

Obsession is heading for a Netflix release this fall and I got an early look, and it's the kind of horror that mistakes fixation for love right up until it can't anymore. No real spoilers from me, just the shape of it: someone circling the same person, the same house, the same idea, refusing to let the obsession cool down. That's basically every training log I've ever kept, if I'm honest, minus the part where anyone gets hurt.

What stuck with me is the pacing. It doesn't rush toward its worst moments, it just keeps returning to them, a little closer each time, like someone working the same three inches of ring space until you've forgotten there was ever anywhere else to stand.

The Odyssey (2026)

Christopher Nolan doing Homer was always going to be an endurance event before it was anything else, and that's exactly how it plays. Matt Damon's Odysseus doesn't get a heroic sprint home, he gets a decade of attrition, and the film is more interested in the toll of the distance than in any single monster along the way. I watched it the same week I did a long slow run I had no business enjoying as much as I did, and something about ten years at sea made forty minutes on a towpath feel almost reasonable.

It's long and it knows it's long, and I mean that as a compliment. Going the distance isn't a montage. It's just a lot of unremarkable miles that happen to end somewhere that matters.

The Patience of a Predator

The Whisper Man puts a grieving father and his young son in Featherbank, a town that's spent fifteen years trying to forget a serial killer who worked through walls and half-heard voices, with rumors that he never worked alone. Nobody in this film gets chased down a hallway. Nobody needs to be. The Whisper Man's whole method is proximity and repetition, testing the same crack in a child's routine, a bedroom wall, a moment alone, over and over, until something finally gives.

That's the part that stuck with me on a week built almost entirely out of jabs. Not the fight-ending shot, the other one. The one that lands eighty times a round and looks like nothing on the tape, the one your corner never mentions because it's not a highlight, it's a habit. I've spent enough dead hours on the bag doing the same six-inch punch to know that most of what actually wins a fight isn't visible from ringside. It's the accumulation. It's the same probe thrown from the same angle until the opponent's guard drops half an inch lower than it did in round one, and by round eight that half inch is the whole fight.

What The Whisper Man understands, and what a lot of chase-and-jump-scare horror doesn't bother with, is that dread built on repetition is worse than dread built on speed. You can outrun a monster in a hallway. You can't out-think something that's willing to try the same door every night for a month. That's not a jump scare, that's a corner-man's nightmare: the fight that won't let you check the exits, round after round, because the danger isn't a single moment, it's the accumulation of moments you didn't notice mattering until they did.

I think about this most on long runs, honestly, more than in sparring. There's a specific low-grade dread on mile nine of a training run when your body starts negotiating with you, quietly, reasonably, the same argument every half mile, wearing down the same door. You don't beat that with a big moment. You beat it by refusing to notice the four hundredth knock any differently than you noticed the first. The journeyman's whole career is built on that same refusal. Nobody claps for the guy who goes twelve rounds on repetition and discipline instead of one clean knockout, but he's usually the one still standing when the film, or the fight, finally ends.

The Whisper Man never lets you forget that patience is its own kind of violence. It's just slower, and quieter, and a lot harder to see coming until you're the one holding the door.

That's the week. Bag work, roadwork, a courtroom cartoon, a killer with better patience than most of my sparring partners. Same time next week, hands still heavy.
