One More Fight: On Final Girls, Journeymen, and the Rounds We Keep Signing Up For

This week's training log: dawn miles, four-round sparring, and a slasher that understands why fighters keep coming back

2026-08-28 · Joshua Laurie
horrorboxingrunning

Six-thirty this morning, still dark, and I'm doing strides on the towpath because my coach thinks I've gotten lazy with my turnover in the championship rounds. Strides are the worst kind of honest: no distance to hide in, just you finding out in real time whether your legs still know how to sprint or whether they've forgotten. Then straight to the gym for four rounds with a journeyman named Deshawn who has fought everyone in a hundred mile radius twice and looks, at thirty-eight, like a man who has personally negotiated a peace treaty with pain. He doesn't hit hard. He hits constantly, which is worse, and it teaches you something about the difference between a knockout puncher and someone who has simply decided he is not going anywhere.

That's the week I brought to the couch. Not a lot of new horror on the release slate, so I let the boxing brain do the sorting: who's built to go the distance, who's running from something they can't outpace, and who got dragged back into a ring they thought they'd already left for good.

Spider-Man: Brand New Day (2026)

There is a version of Peter Parker who has fought so many rounds against the same handful of problems that his footwork has become instinct and his heart has gone somewhere else entirely. That's the guy I watched this week. The web-slinging is as sharp as ever, all lead feet and change of angle, the kind of movement I try to steal for the ring even though nobody swings from a lamppost mid-combination. But the sadness sits underneath it like a bruise you keep re-aggravating in sparring. You can be technically flawless and still be losing on a different scorecard.

I don't need my superhero movies miserable. I just noticed it, the way you notice a fighter who's winning every exchange but has stopped enjoying the fight.

The Odyssey (2026)

This is a film about a man trying to get home and it runs like one too: long, punishing, occasionally beautiful, structured less like a plot than like a training block where the goal keeps receding every time you think you're close. I watched it the same week I ran my longest loop of the summer and felt the same thing at mile fourteen that I felt around the two-hour mark of the movie, which is the specific fatigue of realizing the finish isn't a reward, it's just where the next thing starts.

There's a real discipline in making endurance the plot rather than the backdrop. Most films about a journey cut the boring miles out. This one makes you run them.

The End of Oak Street (2026)

David Robert Mitchell making a survival picture about outrunning something enormous and stupid and relentless is, on paper, exactly my kind of Tuesday. And there's a stretch in the middle, all sprinting through underbrush and doubling back on your own scent, that plays like the most honest depiction of interval training I've seen all year. Sprint, hide, recover, repeat, and pray your legs have one more gear left when the thing finds you again.

It loses me a little as a story. There's a version of this where the tension escalates instead of just repeating, the way a good sparring session builds rather than resets each round. But as pure cardio anxiety, as a film about what it feels like when your body is the only plan you've got left, it worked on me.

Obsession (2025)

A film about fixation ought to feel like the twentieth round of the same drill, and this one does, in a good way. There's a repetition to it, the camera returning to the same faces and rooms and gestures like a fighter running the same three-punch combination into muscle memory until it stops being a choice and starts being who you are.

I recognized the compulsion in it more than I expected to. Training every day isn't really about discipline past a certain point, it's about needing the repetition the way this film's characters need each other, badly and past the point of good sense.

The Final Girl Comes Back for One More Fight

Every gym has a version of Billy Presley. The fighter who already survived the fight everyone talks about, who took the beating that made his name, who walked away from it once and swore that was enough. And every few years someone with a poster and a paycheck shows up asking him to do it again, because the thing that made him a story in the first place was never his happiness. It was his durability.

Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma, Jane Schoenbrun's meta-slasher, hangs its whole engine on that exact request. A young queer director named Kris, played by Hannah Einbinder, is hired to reboot a transphobic eighties slasher franchise, and her first move is convincing the original film's final girl, Billy Presley, to reprise the role decades later. It's billed plainly as horror, comedy, and romance, which undersells how precisely it understands the specific cruelty of asking a survivor to survive again on demand, for someone else's nostalgia this time instead of her own life.

I have sparred with a version of this arrangement more times than I'd like to admit. You take a beating, you learn something from it, you walk out the other side still standing, and that becomes the story people want repeated. Not the growth. The beating. Promoters don't book the guy who figured something out in round nine. They book the guy who can take round nine again, on schedule, for a crowd that wasn't there the first time. Your scar tissue becomes the ticket price. Nobody's paying to see you healed. They're paying to see you reopened.

That's the whole cruel math of the comeback fight, in the ring or on a slasher poster: the corner doesn't care that you already survived this exact fight once before. It just wants to know you can go the distance again, on command, tonight. What makes the film sharper than the average nostalgia cash-in is that Kris herself, the director doing the dragging, effectively becomes a kind of final girl by the end, which is the part that actually rings true to the gym. The trainers and matchmakers who keep calling you back for one more are rarely villains. They're often just people who once made the exact same bargain themselves and never quite got out of the ring either. Everybody in this business, including horror franchises, is a journeyman calling other journeymen back in.

I don't think that's cynical, necessarily. Some fighters do come back better, sharper, on their own terms, and turn the demand into something they actually want. But the film is honest that this is rare, and that most of the time the ask is just the ask: put the body back on the line, we'll pay for the footage. Loving something that never loved you back is a very specific kind of endurance training, and Schoenbrun clearly knows the sport.

Roadwork tomorrow at first light, then rounds in the afternoon with Deshawn again, who I am starting to suspect is training me more than the other way around. See you next Friday.
